# The Big Six-Seven: a field guide to how you work

*Big Five personality + work interests + personal values. The name is a joke. The questions deserve care.*

> Editorial draft for `/articles/big-personality`. The assessment described below is specified but not yet live. Replace implementation-dependent promises only after the corresponding release checks pass.

Early in a career in tech, it is easy to confuse the work you are doing with the person you are becoming. A project goes well and you decide you are a natural leader. A meeting goes badly and you decide you are bad with people. You enjoy one week of debugging and wonder whether you should give up product work. One unusually good or difficult week starts to feel like evidence about your whole identity.

A more useful starting point is to look for patterns, then test what they mean in ordinary life. Which situations reliably engage you? How do you tend to respond to uncertainty? What do you enjoy doing, even when you are still learning? What conditions make your work sustainable?

This experience brings several kinds of evidence into that conversation. It begins with an established Big Five questionnaire, adds separate work-interest and personal-values profiles, and ends with an experiment you choose. You get a report to reflect on, not a verdict about who you are allowed to become.

## Five traits, many ways to be a person

The Big Five describes broad differences in personality. The version used here measures five domains and six narrower facets within each domain. Think of the domains as a first look and the facets as ways to make that look more specific. A broad score can hide differences that matter to you.

| Domain | What it describes in this questionnaire | A question you might explore afterward |
|---|---|---|
| Openness to experience | Tendencies involving imagination, ideas, aesthetics, and openness to experiences | Where does exploration improve my work, and where would a familiar approach help? |
| Conscientiousness | Tendencies involving organization, persistence, deliberation, and responsibility | Which structures help me follow through? |
| Extraversion | Tendencies involving social engagement, assertiveness, activity, and positive emotion | When do conversation and quiet work each help me think? |
| Agreeableness | Tendencies involving cooperation, trust, consideration, and interpersonal approach | How do I combine care for people with an honest disagreement? |
| Neuroticism | Tendencies involving negative emotion and responses to stress | What situations make demands on my coping, and what support helps? |

Those questions are reflection prompts, not conclusions encoded in the questionnaire. A score does not tell us your skill level, your diagnosis, your intelligence, or whether you belong in engineering. There is no ideal developer-shaped profile.

You can be socially expressive and enjoy programming. You can prefer quiet work and become a thoughtful manager. You can like novel ideas and still need a checklist. The interesting question is what you notice when you put a description beside your actual experience.

## Why the sixth lens is work interests

Personality helps describe patterns in how you tend to behave. It does not fully answer which activities attract you. Two people with similar personality scores may be drawn toward different kinds of work.

For that reason, the sixth lens is **vocational interests**, measured separately using the O*NET Mini Interest Profiler. It describes six broad areas commonly called RIASEC: Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, and Conventional. Their names can sound more restrictive than the activities they represent. Read the activities and the profile before deciding that a label fits or does not fit. [O*NET Interest Profiler](https://www.onetcenter.org/IP.html).

The name **The Big Six-Seven** is a deliberate nod to the [6-7 meme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/6-7). Under the joke is a precise structure: five personality traits, a sixth lens on work interests, and a seventh on personal values. We are not claiming a newly established seven-factor personality theory. The interest and values profiles each contain several dimensions; neither becomes one extra personality score.

There is research behind adding this kind of information. One study followed 3,023 German students for ten years and found that vocational interests provided predictive information beyond Big Five traits and IQ for several later outcomes. That supports looking at interests as an additional lens; it does not prove a specific career recommendation for an individual engineer. [Stoll and colleagues, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27560608/).

Interests and personality are related. We are not promising a measurement with zero overlap. We are asking a meaningfully different question.

## What interests might help you explore in tech

The activities in the interests questionnaire span many kinds of work. They have deliberately not been rewritten as a list of software tasks. Changing the questions would change what has been studied.

Afterward, you can use the profile to choose small explorations. Someone drawn toward Investigative activities might try a deeper debugging investigation or an exploratory research task. Artistic interests could inspire interface experiments, visual storytelling, or generative prototypes. Social interests might motivate mentoring or user education. Enterprising interests could make presenting a proposal interesting. Conventional interests may find an outlet in organizing information. Realistic interests may lead toward hands-on building, physical computing, or hardware experiments.

These examples are editorial suggestions, not a validated mapping from a score to an occupation. Most real roles contain several kinds of activity. You also do not need to like every part of your job. The point is to notice what you would like to explore, then get direct experience.

Interest is not ability. You can enjoy an activity you have not yet learned to do well. You can be skilled at something you would prefer to do less often. Opportunity, pay, health, responsibilities, and team conditions also shape what choices are possible.

## The seventh lens: personal values

Enjoying a kind of work does not tell us everything about why it matters to you. Two people may both enjoy investigating a difficult problem. One may care most about the freedom to choose a direction; another may be motivated by helping someone, achieving something recognized, or making a situation more secure. Those differences are worth asking about directly.

The seventh lens is **Personal Values**, measured with the established **Twenty-Item Values Inventory (TwIVI)**. It is based on Schwartz's theory of basic human values and covers ten priorities: self-direction, stimulation, hedonism, achievement, power, security, conformity, tradition, benevolence and universalism. Some names have ordinary-language baggage. Read each definition and your actual answers before turning a label into a judgment. [Instrument and scoring](https://gosling.psy.utexas.edu/two-short-measures-of-values-tivi-and-twivi/).

The questionnaire gives you short portraits of people and asks how similar they are to you. There are 20 portraits and six response options. These are dedicated questions; your values are not guessed from your personality or your interests. The original development used separate samples of 38,049 and 29,143 respondents. That is substantial evidence for a brief values measure, although two questions per value cannot supply a perfectly precise portrait of an individual. [Sandy and colleagues, 2016/2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/00223891.2016.1231115).

What makes this lens different is the question it asks. Personality concerns typical tendencies. Interests concern the attraction of activities. Values concern the goals and principles you prioritize. They can relate to one another. We will not call them independent, or claim that this particular combined questionnaire has already demonstrated added predictive value in tech workers.

The report shows a value's score relative to your own average across all 20 portraits. A positive result means you endorsed that value more than your overall average. A negative result means less than your average; it does not mean you lack that value. You can also see the original 1–6 means. If your answers are all the same, the profile has no differentiated priority. It does not mean you have no values.

Then comes the work reflection, clearly separated from the measured score. You might value self-direction and security at the same time. What would a role with both look like? What is one trade-off you would accept, and one you would not? This is where a general values profile becomes a useful conversation about your current circumstances. It is not a moral ranking or a test of whether you are a good employee.

## Before you answer

Describe how you generally tend to be, rather than an ideal version of yourself or only the last difficult week. Read each statement on its own. Some items are phrased in opposite directions; you do not need to work out how scoring works while answering.

The personality section uses the 120 public-domain items of the **IPIP-NEO-120 (Johnson, 2014)**. The separate interest section has 30 activity questions, and the values section adds 20 portraits: 170 items in the full experience. You can pause and come back; after “Saved on this device” appears, refreshing restores your saved answers and position. Some personality statements refer to negative feelings or political attitudes. You can skip any item, though a skipped answer leaves the affected personality scale incomplete; the interests profile waits until all 30 interest answers are complete, and the values profile waits for all 20 values answers. [IPIP item source](https://ipip.ori.org/30FacetNEO-PI-RItems.htm).

When you reach the interests section, the question changes. You will rate how much you would like activities, following the profiler's instructions, rather than how accurately a personality statement describes you. The values section then changes to six-point portrait-similarity ratings. The response scale is always shown; there is no preselected answer.

No answer makes you a better person. We will not reward a particular response with a congratulatory sound or tell you to optimize your profile.

## How to read the report

The first release shows scores on the questionnaire's response scale. A mean of 3.6 out of 5 is a summary of keyed answers. It is not the 72nd percentile, and it does not mean you have 72% of a trait.

A percentile needs a defensible comparison group and a matching scoring procedure. People who happen to visit a blog are not automatically a representative sample of engineers or the general population. Until we have appropriate reference data, the report will not claim a population ranking. [IPIP guidance on norms](https://ipip.ori.org/newNorms.htm).

Read a trait description alongside its facets, then ask whether it fits. Look for a recent example and a counterexample. Differences may reflect context, a changing routine, how you interpreted a question, or ordinary measurement uncertainty. You can write that a description does not fit you.

The report offers experiments rather than instructions to become someone else. If you tend to speak early in discussions, try writing down your initial idea before a meeting and inviting someone else's perspective first. If you tend to wait, try sharing one provisional thought before it feels finished. Either experiment may help; the observation afterward matters more than the label beforehand.

Choose one experiment that is small enough to try for two weeks. Write down what you will do, the situation in which you will do it, and what you want to notice. You might track focus, enjoyment, ease of collaboration, or whether a habit helped you complete something that matters to you. You do not need a higher personality score to have learned something useful.

## Your report, on your device

The planned experience saves responses in your browser and creates your report there. There is no required account. Once its offline files have been deliberately downloaded, you can finish without a connection. Browser storage can still be removed by you, by private-browsing behavior, or by the browser, so a downloadable personal backup is useful.

Local storage is not a promise that no one using your computer can see your results. It also does not mean the website host never receives a normal page request. The important product promise is narrower and testable: questionnaire answers and private reflections are not automatically submitted to a server.

You can download a PDF for yourself, and you decide what to include. Sharing is a separate action with a preview. A summary link contains only the scores you chose to make public. An exact snapshot link includes your questionnaire answers and position so someone can open that same assessment state and version of the report. It opens read-only and leaves their own saved draft alone; they can choose to create a separate local copy. Private reflection notes stay outside snapshot links. A link in a social post is public to anyone who obtains it; encoding its contents does not make it secret. Exact links use the part after `#`, which is not sent in the ordinary page request. The optional query-parameter summary links can appear in hosting and platform logs. Both kinds of link can be read by anyone who receives them. No remote report database or link-shortening service is required. Deleting your local copy cannot retract a link or a file someone has already received.

If you prefer, share the article without sharing any results. Participating in a community does not require publishing your personality profile.

## What this can and cannot do

An established questionnaire gives us a better starting point than inventing a hundred questions and assuming they measure what their author intended. It still takes work to evaluate a new delivery format, interpretation, language, and audience. The initial release should be described as a research-informed reflection tool, with its evidence and limitations available to read.

We will keep the questions, scoring rules, instrument versions, and interpretation boundaries visible. We will keep personality, interests, and values separate. And we will treat your own observations as something to learn from.

The report is a beginning. The next piece of evidence is what you notice when you try something in your life.
